**[The hidden profit in your unused gift card]**

You’ve been told gift cards expire, but Starbucks’ cards never do. _(s1-1, c2)_

Every unused dollar stays on the retailer’s ledger. _(s1-1a, c2)_

**[When estimates miss the mark]**

A half‑percentage swing in Darden’s breakage estimate would shift reported breakage income. The shift would be $3.6 million for fiscal 2026, according to its filing. _(s5-1, c6)_

That $3.6 million shift moves money from a future liability into current profit. _(s5-3, c6)_

The filing warns that actual breakage could be higher or lower than the recorded amount. Consumer behavior, economic shifts, or legal changes can alter redemption patterns over the twelve‑year horizon. _(s5-4, c4)_

The breakage figure is an estimate. If unredeemed balance later falls under unclaimed‑property law, the company may have to turn it over to the state. That reduces the net profit the company finally keeps. _(s5-5, c4)_


**[The state’s claim on unclaimed balances]**

Federal law says a store gift card can only expire if the balance stays valid for at least five years after it is issued or last loaded. A dormancy or service fee may appear only after a full year of inactivity, and no more than one fee per calendar month. Those rules keep retailers from instantly converting every unused cent into revenue. _(s6-1, c7)_

Darden notes that unclaimed‑property rules are among the accounting and legal requirements that are highly complex. The company must make subjective assumptions and periodic estimates to comply. _(s6-2, c10)_
