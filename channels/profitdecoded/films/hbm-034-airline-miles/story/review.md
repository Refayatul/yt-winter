## Story plan
- **Thesis:** When a bank pays an airline billions for miles, it is buying a currency the airline issues: the airline books part of the money, owes the rest as future seats, forecasts how much will never be claimed, can borrow against the program, and sets what each mile is worth.
- **Central question:** What does a bank actually buy when it pays an airline billions for miles, and what does that mean for the miles in your account?
- **Conflict:** wants Members want miles to keep the value they earned; airlines want card partners to keep buying miles and want the freedom to price awards; obstacle The airline writes the rules of its own currency and can change what a mile buys; the government has asked whether members get the value they were promised; stakes About $27.6 billion of unredeemed-mile balances on three airlines' books (our sum), and the value of the miles viewers already hold
- **Misconception:** That miles are a free thank-you for flying, paid for out of the airline's pocket.
- **Original angle:** Follow one mile from a grocery-store card swipe to the airline's balance sheet with the 2025 filings of Delta, United and American: who pays for it, how it is booked, how much is still owed, what lenders accepted as collateral in 2020, and why the Transportation Department asked whether members get the value they were promised. It uses evidence filed after the large competitor videos were published (FY2025 10-Ks, the September 2024 inquiry).
- **Payoff:** A bank paying for miles is buying a currency the airline issues, plus card perks and use of the airline's name, and hands the miles to cardholders. The airline books part of the money, owes the award-travel part as future seats (tens of billions of dollars across Delta, American and United at the end of 2025), estimates how much will never be redeemed, has borrowed against the program, and sets the terms that decide what a mile buys. The miles in your account are a balance in a currency someone else controls.

## Final script


**[Paid, but not for tickets]**

In 2025, American Express paid Delta Air Lines $8.2 billion. _(s1-1, c1)_

American Express wasn't buying plane tickets. It was buying miles, plus perks for its cardholders. Delta's annual filing says the money was related to SkyMiles, its frequent flyer program. _(s1-2, c3)_

Delta expects those payments to grow to $10 billion over the next few years. _(s1-3, c1)_

The bank hands those miles to its customers: people with a Delta card, and members of its own rewards program. Which means you can earn an airline's miles without ever boarding one of its planes. _(s1-4, c2)_

So what exactly is the bank buying, and what does it mean for the miles sitting in your account? _(s1-5, c2)_


**[Miles without flying]**

Delta's filing lists the ways that happens. Members earn miles by spending with credit card companies, retailers, ridesharing apps, car rental firms and hotels. _(s2-2, c2)_

Those partners don't get the miles for free. They buy them from Delta, and payment is typically due every month, based on how many miles Delta sold them. _(s2-3, c2)_

United has the same kind of deal with Chase. United sells it MileagePlus miles, and Chase awards them to cardholders for their spending. _(s2-4, c10)_

United's filing says its mileage revenue from partners outside the airline, including card spending with Chase, went up in 2025. _(s2-5, c10)_

Picture paying for groceries with one of these cards. The miles that land in your account are miles the bank bought from the airline. The question is what the airline does with that money. _(s2-6, c2)_


**[What a mile is on the books]**

When Amex pays, Delta doesn't count the whole payment as revenue on the spot. _(s3-1, c3)_

Its filing splits the money across what the card actually delivers. Award travel. Waived bag fees. Lounge access. Priority boarding. And the use of the Delta brand. _(s3-2, c3)_

One of those pieces is treated differently from the rest. Award travel. _(s3-3, c3)_

Delta defers that part. It sits on the books as something Delta still owes, and it only becomes passenger revenue when someone redeems the miles and actually takes the flight. _(s3-4, c3)_

So a mile from a card is a promise. The airline has been paid for it, and it still owes you whatever that mile will buy. _(s3-5, c3)_


**[How big the promise is]**

Those promises add up. At the end of 2025, Delta's filing put the deferred revenue for SkyMiles at $9.3 billion. _(s4-1, c4)_

And that balance grew during the year. By our arithmetic, the miles Delta issued in 2025 were worth more than the miles its members used. _(s4-2, i1)_

At American Airlines, the balance for its AAdvantage program was about $10.6 billion. _(s4-3, i6)_

Compare that with what American Airlines owed for tickets people had already bought for future flights. That was about $7.2 billion. _(s4-4, i6)_

So the airline owed about $3.4 billion more in miles than in tickets. That gap is our math. _(s4-5, i6)_

Neither number is cash in a drawer. Both are travel the airline has already been paid for and still has to provide. _(s4-6, c8)_

Now add United's MileagePlus balance. By our sum, the three airlines were carrying about $27.6 billion on their books at the end of 2025, for miles their members hadn't used yet. _(s4-7, i4)_


**[Not every mile comes back]**

Plenty of miles do come back. In 2025, Delta's members redeemed miles for about 35 million award tickets. _(s5-1, c5)_

Twelve percent of the distance passengers flew on Delta was on award travel. _(s5-2, c5)_

But airlines also estimate how many miles will never be spent. _(s5-3, c9)_

American Airlines says it uses a statistical model, built on how members have redeemed in the past, to estimate how many miles won't come back. It then counts that value as revenue little by little, as the other miles are redeemed. _(s5-4, c9)_

And that forecast moves reported revenue. The airline says that if it had raised its estimate of unredeemed miles by 10 percent, that year's revenue would have been about $140 million higher. _(s5-5, c9)_

United runs its own statistical model, account by account. Its filing calls the result mileage breakage. _(s5-6, c11)_

Whether your miles disappear depends on the program. American Airlines miles generally stay valid if the account has qualifying activity at least every 24 months. They also stay valid if the member is the primary holder of its co-branded credit card. That's American's rule. These filings don't give the others'. _(s5-7, c9)_


**[Collateral]**

In 2020, two of these programs became something else: collateral. _(s6-1, c14)_

Delta raised $9 billion that year through notes and a loan, secured by assets tied to SkyMiles. _(s6-2, c14)_

United's filings spell out more. It put the program itself behind the borrowing: the brand, the member data, and the accounts its marketing partners pay into. _(s6-3, c13)_

United also put its own price on the program. It took the program's earnings for 2019, measured before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. Then it multiplied them by 12. That came to about $21.9 billion. _(s6-4, c13)_

That's United's own figure, not an independent valuation. Divide it back by 12, and that earnings measure comes to about $1.8 billion. That division is ours. _(s6-5, i5)_

The borrowing was real. United later recorded $6.8 billion of notes and a loan secured by the MileagePlus business. _(s6-6, c13)_

A frequent flyer program had become an asset in its own right, separate from the planes. _(s6-7, c13)_


**[Who sets the exchange rate]**

Owning a currency comes with a power. The issuer decides what it's worth. _(s7-1, c11)_

In September 2024, the US Department of Transportation started asking about that power. It opened an inquiry into the rewards programs of the four largest US airlines: American, Delta, Southwest and United. _(s7-2, c15)_

It asked about four things. Devaluing rewards people had already earned. Hidden or shifting prices. Extra fees. And less competition. _(s7-3, c15)_

Pete Buttigieg led the department when the inquiry opened. He said that, unlike a traditional savings account, these rewards are controlled by a company that can unilaterally change their value. _(s7-4, c15)_

Rohit Chopra, then director of the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, said these programs are often depreciated or changed with little notice. _(s7-5, c16)_

An inquiry is a question, not a verdict. We haven't verified any outcome from it, so we won't claim one. _(s7-6, c15)_

United's own filing touches the same lever from the accounting side. It warns that changes to its program rules or redemption options may materially change both the balance it owes and the revenue it records. _(s7-7, c11)_


**[A thin year]**

One more pair of numbers shows the scale. In 2025, American Airlines Group reported net income of $111 million. _(s8-1, c6)_

That same year, its co-branded credit card and other partners paid it $6.2 billion in cash. _(s8-2, c7)_

You can't subtract one from the other. That cash isn't profit. It pays for miles and perks the airline still has to deliver. What it shows is how much money flowed in from card partners in a year when the whole company's profit was thin. _(s8-3, c6)_


**[What the filings don't say]**

There are limits to what these filings show. None of them tells you how much profit an airline makes on a single mile, or what the miles in a particular account will buy. _(s9-1, c3)_


**[Someone else's currency]**

So when a bank pays an airline billions for miles, it buys the airline's currency, plus card perks and the right to use the airline's name. Then it hands that currency to you for using its card. _(s10-2, c3)_

The airline books part of the money and owes the rest as future award travel. Across these three airlines, the miles members haven't used yet add up to tens of billions of dollars on the books. _(s10-3, i4)_

It forecasts how many miles will never be claimed. And in 2020, Delta and United borrowed billions against their programs. _(s10-4, c14)_

If you earned your miles on a card, a bank paid for them. But the airline still decides what they'll buy, and it can change that while they sit in your account. _(s10-5, c15)_
