## Story plan
- **Thesis:** An unused balance on a non-expiring gift card stays spendable, but the issuer estimates from its own redemption history how much will not be redeemed and books that share as revenue little by little as other cards are redeemed; unclaimed-property laws may send part of it to government agencies, and federal rules block the quickest ways of taking it.
- **Central question:** If a gift card balance is never spent, who ends up with the money: the company, the government, or the holder?
- **Conflict:** wants The holder assumes an unspent balance is simply lost; the issuer wants to recognize the share it expects never to be redeemed; obstacle The cards never expire, so the company cannot wait for a balance to die; it can only predict, and the prediction can be wrong; stakes Hundreds of millions of dollars a year in reported revenue at one company, and an estimated $27 billion sitting with US adults
- **Misconception:** That the store simply keeps an unused balance when the card runs out.
- **Original angle:** Read the issuers' own filings to show that breakage is a forecast booked gradually, not a forfeited balance; separate what the survey estimates from what companies record; state plainly what the filings do not reveal (how much goes to government).
- **Payoff:** On paper, the holder keeps a non-expiring balance: it stays spendable, with no fees. In practice, the issuer books the share it does not expect to be redeemed, gradually and from its own history. Where unclaimed-property laws apply, part may go to government agencies, and the filings do not say how much. Only the holder decides whether a given balance gets spent.

## Final script


**[The card in the drawer]**

By Bankrate's estimate, Americans are still sitting on about $27 billion in gift cards, vouchers and store credit they haven't used. _(s1-1, c8)_

Some of it may be yours. And if that money never gets spent, who keeps it? _(s1-2, c8)_

That figure comes from a survey run online by YouGov in August 2024 with 2,373 adults. 43 percent said they're holding at least one. _(s1-3, c8)_

It's an estimate, not a count, and Bankrate doesn't publish how it calculated it, which means it tells you the scale, not the exact amount. _(s1-4, c8)_

We don't have to guess. Starbucks, and Darden, the company behind Olive Garden, explain it in their annual filings. And the answer is stranger than "the store just keeps it." _(s1-5, c2)_


**[A card with no end date]**

Start with Starbucks. In the US and its other company-operated markets, Starbucks cards have no expiration date and no service fees. _(s2-1, c2)_

Nothing quietly eats the balance. The money just waits. _(s2-2, c2)_

And yet Starbucks still turns part of that waiting money into revenue. _(s2-3, c2)_

The accounting name for it is breakage: the share of card value a company doesn't expect anyone to ever redeem. _(s2-4, c2)_


**[Forecasting what won't be spent]**

But how do you book money that can still be spent? You can't wait for the card to expire. It won't. _(s3-1, c2)_

So Starbucks looks backward. Its filing says it builds redemption rates from historical patterns in each market, including when a card was activated or reloaded, and through which channel. _(s3-2, c2)_

From that history, it estimates how much of the money is never coming back. _(s3-3, c2)_

Here's the clever part. It doesn't book that amount in one go. It books it over time, in proportion to the cards that do get redeemed. _(s3-4, c2)_

Picture someone tapping a card for a latte. As that balance gets spent, a matching sliver of the money the company doesn't expect anyone to spend moves over into revenue. _(s3-5, c2)_

One phrase in that same filing will matter later. The estimate includes "remittance to government agencies under unclaimed property laws, if applicable." _(s3-6, c2)_


**[How big the slice is]**

So what is this worth to Starbucks? In fiscal 2025, it recognized breakage worth $200.4 million in its company-operated stores. _(s4-1, c1)_

Licensed stores added another $22.0 million. _(s4-2, c1)_

Together, by our own math, that's $222.4 million in one year. The year before, the same math gives $207.6 million. For the year before that, $215.0 million. _(s4-3, i1)_

So this isn't a one-off windfall. It's a steady stream, year after year. _(s4-3b, i1)_

At the end of that fiscal year, Starbucks was holding about $1.75 billion in card balances and loyalty Stars combined, up from about $1.72 billion a year earlier. Not all of that is gift card money, but it shows how big the pool is. _(s4-4a, i4)_

A big pool. But look at what flows through it. _(s4-4, i3)_

In that same year, about $15.2 billion went onto Starbucks cards and into its loyalty program, as new cards, reloads and Stars earned. _(s4-5, i3)_

And about $15.2 billion came back out, as card purchases, Star redemptions and breakage. _(s4-6, i3)_

In other words, the money flowing out almost matches the money flowing in. And breakage is only one part of that outflow. _(s4-7, i3)_


**[Twelve years]**

Now cross the street to Darden. Its gift cards also have no expiration dates, and no dormancy fees. _(s5-1, c4)_

And Darden doesn't count a gift card as a sale when you buy it. It records the sale when the card is redeemed. Until then, the money sits on its books as deferred revenue: paid for, but not yet earned. _(s5-1b, c4)_

It makes the same kind of forecast from its own redemption history, estimating the cards “for which redemption is remote.” But its filing adds a detail that changes the picture. It spreads breakage over the expected redemption period, which is generally 12 years. _(s5-2, c4)_

Twelve years. A card bought for a six-year-old's birthday could still be inside that forecast at their high school graduation. _(s5-3, c4)_

And the pool isn't shrinking. In fiscal 2026, people loaded $760.2 million onto Darden gift cards. _(s5-4, c5)_

Redemptions and breakage together took out $751.9 million. _(s5-5, c5)_

More went in than came out. So the pool didn't shrink: it ended the year at $636.7 million, up from $628.8 million. By our math, the balance grew by $7.9 million in a single year. _(s5-6, c5)_

Darden is careful to say it's a forecast. If people redeem differently than it expects, actual breakage income may differ from what it recorded, and it updates its estimates periodically. _(s5-7, c4)_


**[Half a point]**

Here's what turns this from bookkeeping into a story. Breakage isn't counted. It's predicted. _(s6-1, c6)_

It even puts a price on being wrong. Move the breakage-rate estimate by 50 basis points, that's half of one percentage point, and breakage income shifts by about $3.6 million for the year. _(s6-3, c6)_

Up or down. So the money in your drawer isn't just sitting there. It's an input to a forecast, and that forecast moves a company's income. _(s6-4, c6)_


**[The third player]**

Now, back to that phrase about government agencies. It brings a third player into the story. _(s7-1, c2)_

In plain English: where unclaimed-property laws apply, some unspent money may have to go to the government instead, and Starbucks builds that into its estimate. _(s7-2, c2)_

Darden flags the same laws as a hard problem. It lists unclaimed-property laws among the rules it calls highly complex, involving many subjective assumptions, estimates and judgments. _(s7-3, c10)_

What neither filing tells us is how much unspent money actually ends up with the government. So we won't guess. _(s7-4, c10)_


**[What the law protects, and what it can't]**

There's also a floor under all of this, set by a federal rule, Regulation E. _(s8-1, c7)_

A store gift card can carry an expiration date only if its money stays valid for at least five years after it was issued or last loaded. _(s8-2, c7)_

And an inactivity fee is allowed only after a full year with no activity, and no more than one a month. _(s8-3, c7)_

Those rules close off the quickest ways of draining a card. What they can't do is protect a card from its owner. _(s8-4, c7)_

In that same Bankrate survey, 34 percent of adults said they'd lost money through a gift card mistake. _(s8-5, c9)_

One in five adults said they'd let a card expire. That's possible, because not every card works like Starbucks' or Darden's, and federal rules do allow expiry after five years. _(s8-6, c9)_

Seventeen percent had lost a card. And 12 percent saw the store go out of business before they could use it. _(s8-7, c9)_

The amounts aren't trivial, either. In Bankrate's survey, people holding unused value had an average of $244 of it. _(s8-8, c8)_


**[Who keeps the money]**

So, who keeps the money? _(s9-1, c2)_

On paper, with cards like these, you keep the money. No expiration date, no fees. The balance waits. _(s9-2, c4)_

In practice, the company forecasts how much will never come back, using years of its own data, and moves that share into revenue a little at a time as other people spend. _(s9-3, c2)_

Where unclaimed-property laws apply, part of it may go to the government instead. How much, the filings don't say. _(s9-4, c10)_

And all of it rests on a number nobody can know in advance: how many of us never come back. _(s9-5, c4)_

The company can estimate. The government may take a slice. But only the person holding the card decides whether that balance becomes a meal and a coffee, or a line in someone else's annual report. _(s9-6, c2)_

So, about that drawer. Go check it. _(s9-7, c8)_
