# Billions Sit on Unused Gift Cards. Who Keeps the Money?

Final narration script (selected: Claude Opus reference + approved opening + verified-fact edit). 1,180 words, 54 beats.
Timecodes from the Cartesia narration (Carl). Claim ids refer to dossier `hbm-073`; i* = our arithmetic.


## Cold open

**0:00** `s1-1` (c8) By Bankrate's estimate, Americans are still sitting on about $27 billion in gift cards, vouchers and store credit they haven't used.

**0:10** `s1-2` (c8) Some of it may be yours. And if that money never gets spent, who keeps it?

**0:16** `s1-3` (c8) That figure comes from a survey run online by YouGov in August 2024 with 2,373 adults. 43 percent said they're holding at least one.

**0:28** `s1-4` (c8) It's an estimate, not a count, and Bankrate doesn't publish how it calculated it, which means it tells you the scale, not the exact amount.

**0:37** `s1-5` (c2) We don't have to guess. Starbucks, and Darden, the company behind Olive Garden, explain it in their annual filings. And the answer is stranger than "the store just keeps it."


## Starbucks: no expiry, yet revenue

**0:53** `s2-1` (c2) Start with Starbucks. In the US and its other company-operated markets, Starbucks cards have no expiration date and no service fees.

**1:03** `s2-2` (c2) Nothing quietly eats the balance. The money just waits.

**1:08** `s2-3` (c2) And yet Starbucks still turns part of that waiting money into revenue.

**1:13** `s2-4` (c2) The accounting name for it is breakage: the share of card value a company doesn't expect anyone to ever redeem.


## How breakage is booked

**1:21** `s3-1` (c2) But how do you book money that can still be spent? You can't wait for the card to expire. It won't.

**1:29** `s3-2` (c2) So Starbucks looks backward. Its filing says it builds redemption rates from historical patterns in each market, including when a card was activated or reloaded, and through which channel.

**1:41** `s3-3` (c2) From that history, it estimates how much of the money is never coming back.

**1:46** `s3-4` (c2) Here's the clever part. It doesn't book that amount in one go. It books it over time, in proportion to the cards that do get redeemed.

**1:56** `s3-5` (c2) Picture someone tapping a card for a latte. As that balance gets spent, a matching sliver of the money the company doesn't expect anyone to spend moves over into revenue.

**2:07** `s3-6` (c2) One phrase in that same filing will matter later. The estimate includes "remittance to government agencies under unclaimed property laws, if applicable."


## What it is worth to Starbucks

**2:20** `s4-1` (c1) So what is this worth to Starbucks? In fiscal 2025, it recognized breakage worth $200.4 million in its company-operated stores.

**2:31** `s4-2` (c1) Licensed stores added another $22.0 million.

**2:37** `s4-3` (i1) Together, by our own math, that's $222.4 million in one year. The year before, the same math gives $207.6 million. For the year before that, $215.0 million.

**2:55** `s4-3b` (i1) So this isn't a one-off windfall. It's a steady stream, year after year.

**3:01** `s4-4a` (i4) At the end of that fiscal year, Starbucks was holding about $1.75 billion in card balances and loyalty Stars combined, up from about $1.72 billion a year earlier. Not all of that is gift card money, but it shows how big the pool is.

**3:20** `s4-4` (i3) A big pool. But look at what flows through it.

**3:24** `s4-5` (i3) In that same year, about $15.2 billion went onto Starbucks cards and into its loyalty program, as new cards, reloads and Stars earned.

**3:36** `s4-6` (i3) And about $15.2 billion came back out, as card purchases, Star redemptions and breakage.

**3:43** `s4-7` (i3) In other words, the money flowing out almost matches the money flowing in. And breakage is only one part of that outflow.


## Darden: the 12-year forecast

**3:54** `s5-1` (c4) Now cross the street to Darden. Its gift cards also have no expiration dates, and no dormancy fees.

**4:01** `s5-1b` (c4) And Darden doesn't count a gift card as a sale when you buy it. It records the sale when the card is redeemed. Until then, the money sits on its books as deferred revenue: paid for, but not yet earned.

**4:16** `s5-2` (c4) It makes the same kind of forecast from its own redemption history, estimating the cards “for which redemption is remote.” But its filing adds a detail that changes the picture. It spreads breakage over the expected redemption period, which is generally 12 years.

**4:32** `s5-3` (c4) Twelve years. A card bought for a six-year-old's birthday could still be inside that forecast at their high school graduation.

**4:41** `s5-4` (c5) And the pool isn't shrinking. In fiscal 2026, people loaded $760.2 million onto Darden gift cards.

**4:52** `s5-5` (c5) Redemptions and breakage together took out $751.9 million.

**4:59** `s5-6` (c5) More went in than came out. So the pool didn't shrink: it ended the year at $636.7 million, up from $628.8 million. By our math, the balance grew by $7.9 million in a single year.

**5:17** `s5-7` (c4) Darden is careful to say it's a forecast. If people redeem differently than it expects, actual breakage income may differ from what it recorded, and it updates its estimates periodically.


## Predicted, not counted

**5:30** `s6-1` (c6) Here's what turns this from bookkeeping into a story. Breakage isn't counted. It's predicted.

**5:38** `s6-3` (c6) It even puts a price on being wrong. Move the breakage-rate estimate by 50 basis points, that's half of one percentage point, and breakage income shifts by about $3.6 million for the year.

**5:51** `s6-4` (c6) Up or down. So the money in your drawer isn't just sitting there. It's an input to a forecast, and that forecast moves a company's income.


## The third player

**6:03** `s7-1` (c2) Now, back to that phrase about government agencies. It brings a third player into the story.

**6:09** `s7-2` (c2) In plain English: where unclaimed-property laws apply, some unspent money may have to go to the government instead, and Starbucks builds that into its estimate.

**6:20** `s7-3` (c10) Darden flags the same laws as a hard problem. It lists unclaimed-property laws among the rules it calls highly complex, involving many subjective assumptions, estimates and judgments.

**6:34** `s7-4` (c10) What neither filing tells us is how much unspent money actually ends up with the government. So we won't guess.


## The federal floor, and the owner

**6:43** `s8-1` (c7) There's also a floor under all of this, set by a federal rule, Regulation E.

**6:49** `s8-2` (c7) A store gift card can carry an expiration date only if its money stays valid for at least five years after it was issued or last loaded.

**6:58** `s8-3` (c7) And an inactivity fee is allowed only after a full year with no activity, and no more than one a month.

**7:06** `s8-4` (c7) Those rules close off the quickest ways of draining a card. What they can't do is protect a card from its owner.

**7:14** `s8-5` (c9) In that same Bankrate survey, 34 percent of adults said they'd lost money through a gift card mistake.

**7:22** `s8-6` (c9) One in five adults said they'd let a card expire. That's possible, because not every card works like Starbucks' or Darden's, and federal rules do allow expiry after five years.

**7:34** `s8-7` (c9) Seventeen percent had lost a card. And 12 percent saw the store go out of business before they could use it.

**7:41** `s8-8` (c8) The amounts aren't trivial, either. In Bankrate's survey, people holding unused value had an average of $244 of it.


## Who keeps the money?

**7:52** `s9-1` (c2) So, who keeps the money?

**7:55** `s9-2` (c4) On paper, with cards like these, you keep the money. No expiration date, no fees. The balance waits.

**8:04** `s9-3` (c2) In practice, the company forecasts how much will never come back, using years of its own data, and moves that share into revenue a little at a time as other people spend.

**8:15** `s9-4` (c10) Where unclaimed-property laws apply, part of it may go to the government instead. How much, the filings don't say.

**8:24** `s9-5` (c4) And all of it rests on a number nobody can know in advance: how many of us never come back.

**8:31** `s9-6` (c2) The company can estimate. The government may take a slice. But only the person holding the card decides whether that balance becomes a meal and a coffee, or a line in someone else's annual report.

**8:45** `s9-7` (c8) So, about that drawer. Go check it.
