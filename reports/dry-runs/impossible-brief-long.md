**IMPOSSIBLE_QUESTION** — Start with the assumption. One day without it would expose satellites and power grids to more charged particles; the atmosphere would not vanish in a day.

**INITIAL_CONDITIONS** — That is the first effect — but not the biggest. Assume earth's magnetic field switched off for one day.

**FIRST_EFFECT** — The second-order effect is stranger. Earth's magnetic field is already weakening. Its main dipole has faded about 6.3% per century. Fields have flipped 183 times in 83 million years. The last flip was 780,000 years ago. Now switch it off completely, for just one day. Satellites and power grids would take more charged particles. But the air wouldn't vanish in a day; that takes far longer. So we'd survive, but our technology would feel it first. Over the last two centuries the dipole strength has been decreasing at a rate of about 6.3% per century. Measurements since Gauss in 1832 show a relative decay of about 10% over the last 150 years. There have been at least 183 reversals over the last 83 million years, on average once every ~450,000 years. The latest, the Brunhes–Matuyama reversal, occurred 780,000 years ago. Some sources estimate the four most recent reversals took on average 7,000 years. The geodynamo in Earth's liquid outer core generates a field that deflects the solar wind. Earth's magnetic field switched off for one day.

**SCIENCE_EXPLANATION** — And here is where science stops being certain. The governing physics: the geodynamo in Earth's liquid outer core generates a field that deflects the solar wind.

**LIMITS_UNCERTAINTIES** — So what is the answer? The exact timeline depends on how the impossible change happens; the order of effects is what established physics constrains.
